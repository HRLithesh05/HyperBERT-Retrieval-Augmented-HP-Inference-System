Abstract

logical change but remains challenging due to its hierarchical, multi-label, and
highlyimbalancedstructure.Whilepre–GenerativeAIsupervisedencoder-based
models became the de facto standard for large-scale patent classification, recent
advances in large language models (LLMs) raise questions about whether they
can provide complementary capabilities, particularly for rare or weakly repre-
sentedtechnologicalcategories.Inthiswork,weperformasystematiccomparison
of encoder-based classifiers (BERT, SciBERT, and PatentSBERTa) and open-
weight LLMs on a highly imbalanced benchmark dataset (USPTO-70k). We
evaluate LLMs under zero-shot, few-shot, and retrieval-augmented prompting,
and further assess parameter-efficient fine-tuning of the best-performing model.
Our results show that encoder-based models achieve higher aggregate perfor-

Introduction
Patent classification is a foundational task in the organisation and analysis of tech-
nological information. In scientometrics and patentometrics, automated classification
methods are widely used to map technological domains, trace innovation trajecto-
ries,andstudytechnologicalchangeandemergence(HaghighianRoudsarietal.2022;
Kim et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2024; Oh et al. 2020). Accurate assignment of classifica-
tion schemes such as the IPC or CPC is essential for patent offices and downstream
users—including legal practitioners, technology analysts, and policymakers—to sup-
port prior art search, landscape analysis, and monitoring of emerging technologies
(Krestel et al. 2021; Lin and Chou 2025). The rapid growth of global patent filings,
together with the increasing granularity of classification systems, has intensified the
need for accurate and scalable automated classification methods capable of handling
millions ofheterogeneous, multi-label, andhierarchicallystructured documents, while
avoidingsystematicbiasesacrosstechnologicaldomains(Kresteletal.2021;Kamateri
et al. 2024).
Historically, automated patent classification has progressed through several
methodologicalphases.Earlyapproachesreliedonsparsebag-of-wordsrepresentations
andclassicalmachinelearningalgorithms,whichcapturesurface-levellexicalpatterns
but struggle with synonymy, polysemy, and domain-specific terminology (Chen and
Chang 2012; Wu et al. 2010a). Subsequent work incorporated word embeddings and
deep neural architectures such as convolutional and recurrent neural networks, offer-
ing more expressive representations of patent text and improved performance over
traditional pipelines (Li et al. 2018; Haghighian Roudsari et al. 2022). More recently,
transformer-based pre-trained language models such as BERT and domain-adapted
variants like PatentSBERTa have become a strong baseline for patent classification,
achievingrobustperformanceonlarge-scaledatasetsacrossdifferentlevelsoftheIPC
and CPC hierarchies (Lee and Hsiang 2020; Bekamiri et al. 2024). These encoder-
based models remain comparatively efficient to fine-tune and deploy, making them
attractive for large-scale patent analytics and indicator construction.
2
In parallel, recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have reshaped nat-
ural language processing more broadly (Brown et al. 2020; Bommasani et al. 2021).
LLMs are typically trained on massive text corpora and can be applied to down-
stream tasks through prompting, that is, by providing natural-language instructions
and examples rather than task-specific supervised training. This enables zero-shot
classification, where no labeled examples are provided, and few-shot classification,
whereasmallnumberofillustrativeexamplesisincludedintheprompt(Brownetal.
2020). In addition, LLMs can be combined with external document retrieval mech-
anisms—commonly referred to as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)—to ground
predictions in relevant domain-specific text (Lewis et al. 2020).
These capabilities call for a systematic assessment of the benefits that LLMs may
offer for patent classification, especially in sparsely populated or weakly institution-
alised regions of the classification space, as well as an evaluation of whether these
benefits justify their substantially higher computational cost.
Recent studies suggest that, despite their versatility, the effectiveness of LLMs
in highly technical, domain-specific, and large-scale multi-label settings such as CPC
classification remains uncertain (Kamateri et al. 2024; Rafieian and V´azquez 2025).
At the same time, recent work in scientometrics has begun to explore the use of
large language models in research evaluation and knowledge mapping, often finding
that LLMs can complement traditional indicator-based approaches while also intro-
ducing systematic biases and efficiency concerns (Thelwall and Kurt 2025; Thelwall
2025; Thelwall and Yaghi 2025; Schmitt 2024). Moreover, inference with LLMs typi-
cally incurs substantially higher costs in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy
consumption than encoder-based architectures, posing challenges for large-scale oper-
ationaldeployment(Niuetal.2025).Whilefine-tuningcanimproveLLMperformance
on domain-specific tasks, it requires access to open-weight models and introduces
additionalcomputationalandenvironmentalcosts,makingitspracticalvalueanopen
empirical question.
This work addresses these issues by conducting a systematic comparison between
strong encoder-based baselines (BERT, SciBERT, PatentSBERTa) and a diverse set
of open-weight LLMs. We evaluate multiple inference strategies, including zero-shot
prompting, few-shot prompting, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and hybrid
RAG-plus–few-shot configurations, in order to assess how different forms of con-
textualisation affect CPC multi-label classification performance across frequent and
infrequentsubclasses.Inaddition,weapplyparameter-efficientfine-tuningusingLow-
Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al. 2022) to the best-performing LLM to examine
whethertargetedadaptationcannarrowthegapwithdomain-specificencodermodels.
Finally, we quantify computational cost and energy consumption using the CodeCar-
bon framework (Courty et al. 2024), enabling a principled analysis of the trade-off
between predictive performance, scalability, and sustainability. To assess the robust-
ness and generalisability of our findings across institutional contexts, we further
conduct an external evaluation on an independent dataset of European Patent Office
(EPO) patents, without retraining or adapting the models.
Across these evaluations, we obtain four main results. First, supervised encoder-
based models remain highly competitive on frequent CPC subclasses and achieve

superior micro-averaged performance (which aggregates predictions over all labels

Related Work
This section reviews prior work on automated patent classification by focusing on
the evolution of modeling paradigms rather than on specific application domains. We
organize the literature along a methodological trajectory, moving from traditional
machinelearningpipelinestoencoder-basedtransformermodelsand,morerecently,to
largelanguagemodels.Wealsodiscusshowissuessuchaslabelimbalance,hierarchical
structure, and evaluation choices have shaped both model development and empirical
assessment.
From traditional pipelines to encoder-based models
Early work on automatic patent classification relied on classical text classification
pipelines in which patent documents were represented using bag-of-words (BoW) fea-
tures and classified with supervised learners such as support vector machines (SVMs)
(Wu et al. 2010b) or k-nearest neighbours (KNNs) (Murata et al. 2005). Term Fre-
quency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF–IDF) has been the dominant BoW scheme
for encoding titles, abstracts, or claims into high-dimensional sparse vectors. These
representations were typically used for multi-label prediction of IPC or CPC codes
using linear classifiers. Representative approaches include multi-phase and hybrid
pipelines combining TF–IDF, feature selection, and SVM- or KNN-based classifiers,
achieving moderate performance on collections of tens of thousands of patents (Chen
andChang2012;Wuetal.2010a).Despitetheirscalabilityandsimplicity,BoW-based
methods are fundamentally limited by their inability to capture semantic similarity
beyond lexical overlap and by their sensitivity to vocabulary drift in rapidly evolving
technological domains (Turney and Pantel 2010).
4
The introduction of distributed word representations marked a transition from
sparse BoW features to dense, low-dimensional embeddings. Word2vec- and fastText-
style embeddings trained on large patent corpora enabled more semantically mean-
ingful representations of technical terminology and were combined with neural
architectures such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural net-
works (RNNs), gated recurrent units (GRUs), and long short-term memory (LSTM)
networks (Xiao et al. 2018; Risch and Krestel 2019). CNN-based models, in particu-
lar,demonstratedimprovementsoverBoW+SVMpipelinesbycapturinglocaln-gram
patternsinpatenttext(Lietal.2018).Subsequentworkextendedthesearchitectures
tomulti-labelandhierarchicalclassificationsettings(HaghighianRoudsarietal.2022;
Nemati 2024). Nevertheless, these models relied on static word embeddings, limit-
ing their ability to handle polysemy, contextual variation, and evolving terminology,
andoftenstruggledwiththelengthandstructuralheterogeneityofpatentdocuments
(Yang et al. 2016).
The introduction of transformer-based language models marked a substantial
advance in patent classification. Fine-tuning architectures such as BERT and its vari-
ants on patent corpora led to significant performance gains across single-label and
multi-label classification tasks and across multiple levels of the IPC and CPC hierar-
chies (Lee and Hsiang 2020). Domain-adapted variants trained on large-scale patent
collectionsfurtherimprovedperformancebyaligningthepretrainingdistributionwith
patent-specific language and stylistic conventions (Bekamiri et al. 2024; Shen et al.
2023).Asaresult,encoder-basedclassifiershavebecomeadefactostandardforauto-
matedIPCandCPCassignment,owingtotheirstrongperformanceonfrequentlabels
and their computational efficiency at inference time (Lu et al. 2024).
From a patentometrics perspective, encoder models are attractive because they
scale efficiently to millions of documents and can be seamlessly integrated into large
indicator pipelines (Krestel et al. 2021). However, prior studies also note their sen-
sitivity to label imbalance and their tendency to optimize performance on dominant
subclasses (Pujari et al. 2021; Nam et al. 2014). Consequently, macro-averaged and
hierarchical metrics are often substantially lower than micro-averaged scores, rais-
ing concerns about their suitability for analyzing emerging or weakly represented
technological areas (Krestel et al. 2021; Lin and Chou 2025; Kamateri et al. 2024).
Large language models and prompt-based classification
More recently, LLMs have been explored as an alternative paradigm for patent clas-
sification. Unlike encoder-based systems, LLMs can perform zero-shot and few-shot
classification by leveraging instruction-following capabilities acquired during large-
scale pretraining. Initial studies show that LLMs can assign plausible labels without
task-specificfine-tuningandthatperformancecanbeimprovedthroughpromptengi-
neering, few-shot exemplars, and lightweight retrieval augmentation (Yoshikawa and
Krestel 2024; Rafieian and V´azquez 2025; Xiong et al. 2025).
Applications of LLMs to patent analysis are still relatively limited in number and
scope. Parallel discussions in scientometrics emphasize a similar pattern: LLMs are
increasingly explored as assistive tools for evaluation and mapping tasks, but their
reliability, bias, and resource requirements remain open concerns (Thelwall 2025; Sun
5
2025;Cantoneetal.2025;Schmitt2024).Whilerecentstudiesdemonstratepromising
performance in specific settings—often by integrating LLMs into hybrid or assisted
workflowssuchasactivelearning—mostcontributionsremaintask-ordomain-specific
and stop short of providing systematic, large-scale benchmarking across datasets,
prompting strategies, and evaluation dimensions (Kamateri et al. 2024; Xiong et al.
2025). Moreover, despite their relevance for operational deployment in patent offices
and research institutions, the computational cost and energy footprint of LLM-based
approaches are rarely analyzed as first-class evaluation criteria.
Challenges and Responsible Use of Automated Patent
Classification
Patent classification remains intrinsically challenging. Patent documents are long,
heterogeneous, and densely technical; classification systems are highly granular and
hierarchical; and expert human examiners themselves exhibit non-negligible disagree-
ment in code assignment (Burke and Reitzig 2007). As a result, state-of-the-art
automated classifiers often achieve high micro-averaged performance while exhibiting
substantially weaker macro-averaged and rare-label performance, particularly at fine-
grainedsubclasslevels(Kresteletal.2021;LinandChou2025;Kamaterietal.2024).
These limitations have motivated research on ensemble methods, explainability, and,
more recently, the application of LLMs to patent classification (Kamateri et al. 2023;
Rafieian and V´azquez 2025).
Beyondpredictiveaccuracy,recentworkinscientometricsandpatentometricscalls
forgreaterattentiontotheresponsibleuseofautomatedmethods,emphasizingtrans-
parency,robustness,andawarenessofbiasinindicatorconstruction(Hicksetal.2015;
Waltman2016).Atthesametime,theenvironmentalimpactofmachinelearningmod-
elshasbecomeanincreasinglysalientconcernforlarge-scaletextprocessing(Schwartz
et al. 2020). Recent contributions explicitly discuss these concerns in the context of
large language models, highlighting both their analytical potential and the risks asso-
ciated with opacity, bias, and uncritical adoption in evaluative settings (Thelwall and
Kurt 2025; Sun 2025).
However, it remains insufficiently understood how differences between encoder-
basedmodelsandLLMstranslateintosystematicbiasesoradvantagesindownstream
scientometric analyses, particularly in the presence of long-tailed and hierarchical
classification systems. Against this background, the present study provides a sys-
tematic comparison between encoder-based models and LLMs for CPC multi-label
patentclassification.Unlikepriorwork,weevaluatebothparadigmsundercomparable
conditions, explicitly accounting for hierarchical performance, rare-label behaviour,
inference-time efficiency, and energy consumption. By focusing on compact, locally
deployable LLMs and parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies, we assess not only
predictive performance but also the practical and methodological trade-offs faced by
patent offices and scientometric researchers (Kamateri et al. 2024).

Methodology
This study investigates the effectiveness of supervised encoder models and large lan-
guage models (LLMs) for CPC subclass-level multi-label patent classification, with
particular attention to the trade-offs between predictive performance, computational
cost, and environmental impact (Figure 1). To enable a fair and realistic compari-
son, we adopt an experimental pipeline based on a temporally split patent corpus,
preprocessing, standardized evaluation metrics, and systematic tracking of energy
consumption and CO emissions. For encoder-based models, we adopt standard fine-
2
tuning and decoding settings commonly used in the patent classification literature,
obtaining performance in line with previously reported results (Lee and Hsiang 2020;
Bekamiri et al. 2024). In contrast, while the application of LLMs to patent classifica-
tion has so far been limited and largely focused on predictive accuracy, we evaluate
LLMs under a rigorous and controlled experimental protocol that explicitly accounts
forpromptingstrategies,parameter-efficientfine-tuning,inference-timeefficiency,and
energyconsumption—dimensionsthathavereceivedlittlesystematicattentioninprior
work.WefirstestablishstrongsupervisedbaselinesusingTransformer-basedencoders
fine-tuned on labeled patent data. We then evaluate instruction-tuned LLMs under
progressivelymoreinformativepromptingregimes,rangingfromzero-shotinferenceto
retrieval-augmented few-shot prompting that incorporates both labeled examples and
structured CPC definitions. Finally, we select the best-performing LLM configuration
andadaptittothetaskviaparameter-efficientfine-tuningusingLoRA.Thisprogres-
sive design allows us to disentangle the effects of domain pre-training, prompt-based
reasoning, external knowledge injection, and task-specific adaptation, while jointly
assessing accuracy, efficiency, and sustainability.
Dataset
We conduct our experiments on the USPTO-70k dataset (Pujari et al. 2021), a
benchmark for CPC subclass-level multi-label patent classification. The corpus com-
prises70250USPTOpatentdocumentswithachronologicalsplitdesignedtoemulate

textpreprocessing,consistingofbasicnormalization(e.g.,lowercasingandwhitespace
normalization),andensureconsistentformattingacrossallmodelfamilies.CPClabels
are normalized to the four-character subclass level and encoded as multi-hot vectors.
All models are trained and evaluated using the same textual input fields to ensure a
fair and controlled comparison.

Model Selection and LoRA Fine-Tuning
Across all LLMs and prompting configurations, Qwen-2.5-7B achieves the strongest
validation performance (notably in micro- and hierarchical F1), and is therefore
selectedforparameter-efficienttaskadaptationviaLoRA(Low-RankAdaptation)(Hu
et al. 2022). LoRA adapts a pre-trained model by adding trainable low-rank updates
toselectedweightmatrices(typicallyintheattentionand/orfeed-forwardprojections)
while keeping the base weights frozen. Concretely, for a weight matrix W ∈ Rd×k,
LoRA learns an additive update ∆W parameterized as a rank-r decomposition:
W′ =W+∆W, ∆W=BA, (1)
where A ∈ Rr×k and B ∈ Rd×r with r ≪ min(d,k). This substantially reduces
the number of trainable parameters and the optimizer state footprint compared
to full fine-tuning, enabling efficient adaptation of multi-billion-parameter models.
Implementation details are available in Appendix D.

The evaluation of large-scale, multi-label patent classification systems requires
careful interpretation of aggregate performance metrics, particularly in the presence
of strong imbalances in CPC subclass frequencies. We therefore distinguish between
overall performance assessed over the full label space and stratified analyses focusing
on frequent and rare subclasses.
For aggregate metrics computed over all CPC subclasses (e.g., micro- and macro-
averagedprecision,recall,andF1),weadoptaprimarilydescriptiveperspective.Point
estimates are complemented with non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals to
quantify uncertainty. In particular, CPC subclasses are resampled with replacement,
andthecorrespondingaggregatemetricsarerecomputedover1000bootstrapsamples
(Efron and Tibshirani 1993). This procedure captures variability across technological
categories without relying on distributional assumptions. Formal hypothesis testing
is not applied to overall metrics, as these comparisons are dominated by frequent
subclasses and are not associated with a specific null hypothesis.
To investigate performance differences across technological regimes, CPC sub-
classes are partitioned according to their empirical frequency in the evaluation data.
Subclasses in the top 20% of the support distribution are treated as frequent, while
thoseinthebottom20%aretreatedasrare.Thisstratificationenablesafocusedanal-
ysisofmodelbehavioronwell-establishedversussparselyrepresentedtechnologiesand
supports the interpretation of macro-averaged results.

Data Description
The patent classification task considered in this study is characterized by a highly
imbalanced and multi-label output space. Each patent is annotated with one or more
Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) subclasses at the four-character level (e.g.,
G06F, H04L), resulting in a large and sparse label vocabulary.
Figure 2a reports the distribution of CPC subclasses in the training set, ordered
by decreasing frequency. The distribution exhibits a pronounced long-tail structure: a
small number of subclasses account for a substantial fraction of all label assignments,
whilethemajorityofsubclassesappearonlyrarely.Thisimbalanceisfurtherquantified
in Figure 2b, which shows that a limited fraction of subclasses covers most of the
labeled instances.
Inaddition,patentsareinherentlymulti-labeled.Figure2creportsthedistribution
of the number of CPC subclasses per patent in both the training and test sets. The

statisticsoftheCPClabelsinthetestandtrainingsetsareprovidedinAppendixE.1.
The extreme skewness of the label distribution has important implications for

motivates the use of complementary evaluation perspectives and provides context for

Energy–Accuracy Trade-off
Whileclassificationperformanceprovidesonedimensionofcomparison,Table3reveals
pronounced differences in computational cost across model families. Encoder-based

based approaches require between 30 and 280 minutes per run and consume 0.2–2.1

efficient at inference time.
Zero-shot LLM configurations are often particularly unfavorable, especially for
larger instruction-tuned models, as they combine relatively low classification per-
formance with high computational cost. For instance, models such as LLaMA-3.1-

outperform encoder baselines in Micro-F1, placing them in an inefficient region of the
accuracy–energy trade-off space (Figure 4).

A central empirical finding of this study is that LLMs outperform encoder-based

prior research associates with higher impact and disruptiveness (Xu et al. 2025; Wu
et al. 2024). Although this comes at some cost to precision, the improved recall sug-
gests that LLMs can play a complementary role to encoder-based systems focused on
established technological domains.

Beyond operational settings, these findings have broader implications for sci-

large language models in research evaluation, which underline both their potential to

Future Work
This study is methodological and comparative in nature, with the primary goal of
systematically assessing the behavior of (pre-GenAI) encoder-based models and large
languagemodelsforpatentclassificationunderrealisticandcontrolledconditions.Sev-
eral observations emerging from this analysis naturally point to promising directions
for future research.
22
First, the comparative evidence presented in this paper indicates complementary
strengths of encoder-based classifiers and LLMs across different regions of the CPC
label space. In particular, the results suggest the potential usefulness of an innovative
hybrid methodology that integrates encoder- and LLM-based approaches to leverage
the efficiency and stability of encoders with the flexibility and the ability to focus on
emerging subclasses of LLMs. While this insight is directly informed by our findings,
the design, implementation, and empirical evaluation of such hybrid systems would
be beyond the scope of the present study and are left for future work.
Second, by design choice, our analysis focuses on instruction-tuned LLMs in the
7–8B parameter range. This choice reflects a methodological commitment to fair
and meaningful comparison under realistic deployment constraints. Future work may
nonetheless explore the performance–cost trade-offs across a broader range of model
sizes,consideringalsoapproachesbasedonmodelreductionsuchasquantization(Gray
and Neuhoff 1998).
Third,wediscussedhowfine-tuningthebest-performingLLMdidnotconsistently
leadtoperformanceimprovements.While,asdiscussed,thisbehaviorhasalreadybeen
observed in the literature, future work could consider other fine-tuning approaches,
including aggressive full fine-tuning, not feasible with the computational resources
available for this study.
Finally, while the retrieval-augmented prompting strategies explored in this work
rely on official CPC subclass definitions, richer forms of retrieval and interaction may
further improve practical usefulness. Promising extensions include adaptive retrieval
over heterogeneous knowledge sources and human-in-the-loop evaluation to assess
how LLM-generated suggestions are used by patent examiners in real workflows. In
this broader context, the development of cost-aware hybrid systems that dynami-
callybalanceencoderpredictions,LLMassistance,andhumanoversightrepresentsan
especially compelling direction for future research.
Conclusion
Thisstudyprovidesasystematiccomparisonbetweensupervisedencoder-basedmod-
els and open-source LLMs for CPC multi-label patent classification, with explicit
attention to performance across the CPC hierarchy, long-tailed label distributions,
and computational sustainability. Rather than identifying a single best-performing
approach, the results indicate a consistent trade-off between efficiency and seman-
tic coverage, with direct consequences for how technological domains are represented
in downstream analyses. Encoder-based models remain highly effective for frequent
CPC subclasses and large-scale, high-throughput classification tasks, offering strong
micro-level performance at minimal computational and environmental cost. In con-
trast, LLMs—particularly when augmented with few-shot prompting and lightweight
retrieval—exhibit improved recall on rare and infrequent subclasses and more bal-
anced coverage across the CPC hierarchy, albeit at substantially higher resource
requirements.
These findings have direct implications for both operational patent classification
andscientometricanalysis.ForpatentofficesandIPprofessionals,theysupporthybrid

Appendix A Supervised encoder models:
implementation details
A.1 Model architecture and training
Each encoder model (BERT, SciBERT, PatentSBERTa) is fine-tuned for multi-label
CPC classification by attaching a linear classification head that outputs one logit per
CPC subclass. Logits are transformed into independent probabilities via a sigmoid
function,andmodelsaretrainedusingbinarycross-entropylossoverthefulllabelset.
All models are trained on the same training split and evaluated on a held-out
test set. Input sequences consist of the patent title concatenated with the abstract
and are truncated to a maximum length of 256 tokens. Training is performed using
standardoptimizationsettings,andthefinalcheckpointisselectedbasedonvalidation
performance.
A.2 Threshold calibration and label decoding
For each encoder, prediction thresholds are calibrated on a separate development set
to maximize micro-averaged F score. At test time, a CPC subclass is predicted for
1
a patent if its calibrated probability exceeds the model-specific threshold. To ensure
comparability with LLM outputs, we additionally cap the number of predicted labels
per patent to at most seven; if no label exceeds the threshold, the highest-probability
subclassisselected.Infact,thenumberofCPCsubclassesassignedperpatentishighly
sparse. In the training set (50625 patents), the mean number of labels per patent is
1.98 and the median is 2; 90% of patents have at most four labels, 95% have at most
four labels, and 99% have at most seven labels. Overall, 99.48% of training patents
are annotated with between one and seven CPC subclasses, with a maximum of 18.
The test set exhibits a similar distribution (mean: 2.32; median: 2; 99th per-
centile: 7), with 99.21% of patents having between one and seven labels. Based on
this empirical distribution, we restrict model outputs to at most seven subclasses per
patentacrossallmethods.Thisconstraintisapplieduniformlytosupervisedencoders
and LLM-based approaches and serves to prevent degenerate over-prediction while
remaining faithful to the annotation regime.
A.3 Constrained label-space evaluation
In addition to standard evaluation over the full CPC subclass space, we consider a
constrained decoding setting that mirrors label-space retrieval prompting used for
LLMs. For each patent, a per-instance candidate set of CPC subclasses is retrieved
by embedding official CPC subclass definitions with a bi-encoder (E5-base-v2) and
ranking them by cosine similarity to the patent text.
During constrained evaluation, encoder output probabilities for subclasses outside
theretrievedtop-K set(K =20)aremaskedtozeropriortothresholdinganddecod-
ing.ThevalueK = 20 controlsonlythesizeoftheretrievedcandidatelabelset;after
thresholding,thenumberofpredictedCPCsubclassesisstillcappedatseven,Impor-
tantly, this constraint is applied only at inference time and does not influence model
training or parameter estimation.

Appendix B Prompting Strategies and Templates
We evaluate large language models under multiple prompting regimes of increas-
ing informational content and constraint, reflecting common practical deployment
scenarios. Specifically, we consider: (i) zero-shot prompting; (ii) few-shot prompt-
ing with in-context examples; (iii) constrained prompting based on an allowed CPC
label set, implemented via retrieval from a dictionary of CPC definitions (retrieval-
augmented prompting); and (iv) combinations of few-shot prompting with label-set
constraints.Acrossallsettings,promptsenforceastrictJSONoutputformattoensure
deterministic parsing and consistent evaluation.
B.1 Prompt selection and calibration
Prior to the main experimental evaluation, we conducted a limited prompt calibra-
tion phase on a small validation subset of the training data. During this phase, we
tested several semantically equivalent prompt variants differing in wording, emphasis
on recall versus precision, and degree of explicitness regarding multi-label prediction.
This preliminary exploration was motivated by empirical observations that large lan-
guage models tend to be conservative in multi-label settings, often under-predicting
the number of relevant labels when instructions are underspecified.
Importantly, prompt selection was performed once per prompting regime (e.g.,
zero-shot,few-shot,constrained)andexclusivelyonheld-outvalidationdata.Thefinal
prompt templates reported in this appendix correspond to the variants that yielded
the best overall performance in terms of micro-F1 and recall, while preserving strict
outputformattingandcomparabilityacrossmodels.Nopromptwastunedonthetest
set, and the same finalized prompts were used unchanged for all models within each
experimental condition.

tions, without examples or label constraints. This represents the cleanest evaluation
of the model’s prior knowledge.

Appendix C Retrieval and RAG Implementation
Details
This appendix documents the implementation details of our retrieval-augmented
prompting settings used for CPC multi-label prediction.
C.1 Retrieval corpus: CPC subclass definitions
We construct a retrieval corpus from a dictionary of CPC subclass codes mapped to
short textual definitions (denoted cpc labels). Each retrieval item corresponds to
one CPC subclass code (4-character level, e.g., G06F) and its definition. Before
embedding, we normalize codes to the 4-character subclass level by (i) uppercasing,
(ii) removing any subgroup suffix after “/” if present, and (iii) truncating to four
characters. Each retrieval item is embedded as a single text string of the form:
CODE: definition
This design ensures that retrieval is performed over the semantic content of the CPC
definition while preserving explicit label identity.
C.2 Bi-encoder retrieval model and embeddings
WeusealocalE5bi-encoder(e5-base-v2)toembedbothqueriesandCPC-definition
passages. Following the E5 convention, we apply explicit prefixes:
• Query embedding: patent text prefixed with ‘‘query: ’’.
• Passage embedding: CPC definition text prefixed with ‘‘passage: ’’.
The query text is the concatenation of the patent title and abstract (“title.
abstract”), with whitespace normalization. Tokenization uses truncation to a maxi-
mum length of 512 tokens. Embeddings are computed via mean pooling over the last
36
hidden states using the attention mask, and then ℓ -normalized. Because embeddings
2
are normalized, cosine similarity is equivalent to the dot product.
We embed the CPC-definition corpus once (batch size 64) and reuse these vectors
for all patents. For patent queries, we embed in batches (default batch size up to 16,
capped by the number of items in a batch). All embedding computations run locally
(offline), using GPU.
C.3 Top-K retrieval and allowed-label construction
For each patent, we compute cosine similarity between the query embedding and all
CPC-definitionembeddingsandretrievethetop-K CPCsubclasses,withK =20.The
resulting list forms the allowed label set for that patent. This allowed set is injected
into the LLM prompt and also used for strict post-processing: predicted labels not
contained in the allowed set are discarded. We fix the size of the retrieved allowed
CPCsettoK =20acrossallmodelsandexperiments.Weconsideredalternativesizes
oftheretrievedallowedlabelsettoassessfeasibility.WithK =10,theretrievalstage
frequently omitted relevant CPC subclasses, leading to under-inclusive predictions.
Conversely,K =50resultedinsubstantiallylongerpromptsthatapproachedcontext-
lengthlimitsandincreasedinferencecostwithoutqualitativebenefits.Basedonthese
observations, we fixed K =20 a priori and did not further tune it.
C.4 Prompt formatting for RAG settings
In RAG prompting, the allowed CPC subclasses are shown together with short
definitions and grouped hierarchically to improve readability. Specifically, we group
allowed subclasses by CPC section (first character) and class (first three characters),
and list subclasses under each class. Each line is formatted as:
- CODE --- definition
To control prompt length, definitions are truncated to at most 220 characters.
Zero-shot + RAG.
In the zero-shot + RAG condition, the prompt contains only (i) the patent text and
(ii) the retrieved allowed set with definitions. The model is instructed to select all
relevant subclasses from the allowed set and to return a strict JSON object:
{"labels": ["G06F", "H04L"]}
Weadditionallyinstructthemodeltopreferover-inclusion(highrecall)andtooutput
between 1 and 7 labels.
Few-shot + RAG.
In the few-shot + RAG condition, we use the same allowed-set retrieval as above and
prependfew-shotexamplestotheprompt.Few-shotexamplescomefromthetraining
setandareformattedasPATENT TEXTfollowedbythecorrespondinggoldlabelJSON.
We include two types of few-shot examples:
• Static examples: fixedtraining rowindices (two examples) usedin everyprompt.
37
• Dynamic examples: retrieved per test instance using TF–IDF cosine similarity
over training texts (two examples), excluding the static IDs.
We experimented with alternative few-shot configurations (static-only and
dynamic-only retrieval), but observed reduced recall and higher variance across runs;
for this reason, these variants were not pursued further.
C.5 Few-shot retrieval for examples (TF–IDF)
For dynamic few-shot retrieval, we build a TF–IDF representation of training texts
(title + abstract) using: maximum 50000 features, (1,2)-gram range, and minimum
document frequency of 2. For each test patent, we compute cosine similarity between
itsTF–IDF vector andall training vectors andselect thetop-2 mostsimilar examples
(excluding the static examples). Static examples are selected by fixed training indices
[2, 27] to stabilize the prompt format across runs.
C.6 LLM inference settings
All LLMs are run locally (offline) using Hugging Face Transformers with
device map="auto". We use bfloat16 on supported GPUs (otherwise float16).
We generate deterministically with greedy decoding (do sample=False) and
max new tokens=256. Tokenization uses left padding and truncation to a maximum
input length of 2048 tokens.
C.7 Output parsing, label normalization, and constraint
enforcement
We enforce structured output by requiring strict JSON in the prompt. In post-
processing, we parse the model output as follows:
1. Attempt to parse the entire generation as JSON and read the "labels" list.
2. If this fails, extract the first JSON-like substring using a regex and attempt JSON
parsing again.
3. Normalize each predicted code to the 4-character subclass level (uppercasing,
removing subgroup suffix after “/”, truncating to four characters).
For RAG conditions, we enforce the allowed-label constraint by discarding any
normalized label not present in the retrieved allowed set for that patent.
Non-empty fallback.
Because some LLMs may output invalid JSON or produce empty outputs, we option-
ally enforce non-empty predictions. If parsing yields no valid labels, we (i) search for
CPC-like tokens in the raw text and keep the first token that is in the allowed set;
otherwise (ii) fall back to predicting the top-1 retrieved allowed label. In all settings,
wecapthefinalpredictiontoatmost7labelsperpatent.Weconstrainthenumberof
predicted CPC subclasses to be between 1 and 7. This range reflects the typical spar-
sityofCPCsubclassannotationsinourdatasetandpreventsdegenerateoutputswith

excessively many labels, while still allowing over-inclusion in line with our high-recall
objective.

Appendix D LoRA Fine-Tuning Details
We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using parameter-efficient Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA).Themodelistrainedwithacausallanguagemodelingobjective,implemented
as token-level cross-entropy loss over the target output sequence. The task is formu-
lated as structured text generation, where the model outputs CPC subclass labels in
a JSON format.
LoRA adapters are applied while keeping all base model parameters frozen. We
use a rank of r =64, a scaling factor α=16, and a dropout rate of 0.05, with no bias
terms. Adapters are injected into both attention and feed-forward projection layers,
including query, key, value, and output projections in the attention blocks, as well as
the gate, up, and down projection layers in the MLP blocks.
Optimization is performed using AdamW with a peak learning rate of 2×10−4
and a cosine learning rate schedule with a warmup ratio of 0.05. We apply weight
decay of 0.01. Training is carried out for three epochs with a per-device batch size of
2 and gradient accumulation over 8 steps, resulting in an effective batch size of 16.
The maximum sequence length is set to 2048 tokens. Training is conducted in mixed
precision, using bfloat16 when supported by the hardware and float16 otherwise.
Model checkpoints are saved every 500 training steps, and validation is performed
at the same interval. All experiments are run on a single GPU without model quan-
tization. To monitor computational cost, training time and carbon emissions are
tracked using the CodeCarbon framework. All values correspond to standard choices
commonly used in LoRA-based fine-tuning of large language models.
